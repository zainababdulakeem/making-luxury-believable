An essay on how luxury value is formed, and a practical companion for anyone building or studying a brand.

An Organisational Model of Luxury Value Formation

Luxury branding is often criticised for selling an artificial feeling of social superiority rather than meaningful value. Yet this criticism assumes that value must come from the physical usefulness of a product. In reality, consumers also pay for identity, emotion, status and belonging. Luxury branding can create genuine customer value when a brand’s claims become socially credible through consistent experiences, recognisable symbols and acceptance beyond the firm itself. The crucial issue, therefore, is whether the meaning being sold becomes believable enough to matter.

However, a luxury claim cannot become socially meaningful through advertising alone. Its credibility develops through what happens after the claim enters the market. Consumers experience the brand, interpret its symbols, compare it with alternatives and communicate its meaning to others. Over time, these repeated interactions can turn a firm’s intended image into a socially recognised meaning. Research on brand heritage illustrates this process. Wuestefeld et al. (2012), studying 259 users of Chanel, found that perceived brand heritage had significant positive effects on economic, functional, affective and social customer value. Heritage therefore does more than decorate a brand story. When supported by longevity, a strong track record, core values and recognisable symbols, it can become evidence that makes the brand’s promised meaning more credible.

Calling this feeling “artificial,” however, does not necessarily make the value unreal. Social meanings are constructed, but constructed meanings can produce real consequences. Money, status and national identity all depend partly on collective recognition, yet their effects are not imaginary. Luxury works similarly: a brand may promise social superiority, but that promise becomes valuable only when other people recognise the signals through which it is expressed. However, a business cannot create that value simply by claiming that its customers are superior to others. If a firm claims heritage, exclusivity or quality without anything credible behind the claim, it is asking customers to pay for an image that may exist only in its advertising.

Luxury branding is therefore neither simply a psychological trick nor merely a premium attached to a physical product. Its value can emerge through a process in which firms create distinctive experiences, customers interpret them, and those meanings gradually gain recognition in society. Businesses have a legitimate right to charge for this value when the experience they create is credible and consistently delivered. The strongest luxury brands do not merely tell customers that they are buying superiority; they build the conditions that make others believe it too.

Reference

Wuestefeld, T., Hennigs, N., Schmidt, S. and Wiedmann, K.-P. (2012) ‘The impact of brand heritage on customer perceived value’, der markt, 51(2–3), pp. 51–61. doi:10.1007/s12642-012-0074-2.

Practical Applications and Further Development

A companion to the essay. The essay argues that luxury value doesn’t come from a product’s usefulness alone. It develops when a brand’s claims become credible through what the business delivers, how people interpret the experience, and whether the brand’s meaning gains recognition beyond the company itself.

If you’re building a brand, running a business or studying one, here are four questions to ask.

1. Why should anyone believe the claim?

Imagine a new skincare brand launches with elegant packaging, sophisticated advertising and premium prices. It looks like luxury, but customers have little reason to tell its products apart from cheaper ones.

The problem isn’t the branding itself. The brand made a promise before giving customers a reason to believe it.

A company doesn’t need decades of history to build credibility. Product quality, distinctive design, thoughtful service and consistent experiences can all do it. What matters is whether the experience backs up the meaning being communicated.

Put it into practice
  • What does the business promise beyond the product’s basic function?
  • What gives customers reasons to believe that promise?
  • Where does the brand’s presentation promise more than the offering can deliver?

2. How do different customers build one reputation?

Picture three people who own the same handbag. One bought it for the craftsmanship, one for the history behind the name, and one for what carrying it says about them. Their reasons differ, but each interpretation can contribute to the brand’s wider reputation.

For a business, the task is to understand how those different readings add up to a recognisable identity, and whether they support each other or pull in different directions.

Put it into practice
  • What different reasons might customers have for valuing the same offering?
  • Which associations do they share?
  • Does the brand’s reputation reflect a real identity, or just a name people recognise?

3. Can growth weaken what made the brand valuable?

A brand known for exclusivity starts selling through more outlets. It might win new customers, or it might change how existing customers see the brand.

Neither outcome is certain. It depends on what exclusivity means to those customers and how the change affects their experience.

So before expanding, a business should ask what the move does to the things customers value, and not only what it does to sales.

Put it into practice
  • What could this decision strengthen, change or weaken about the brand?
  • Does it build on the value customers already appreciate?
  • Is the business protecting what makes the brand valuable, or just keeping up its appearance?

4. Does recognition turn into revenue?

Imagine someone choosing between two watches that tell time equally well. They admire one brand’s reputation, but the other costs half as much. Some customers will pay the extra. Others will admire the brand and buy the cheaper watch.

Being admired doesn’t guarantee people will keep paying the premium. A business needs to know whether its reputation is actually moving buying decisions or just earning compliments.

Put it into practice
  • What would make customers willing to pay more for this offering?
  • What alternatives could change their decision?
  • Is the brand’s perceived value shaping what people buy, or is recognition not turning into demand?

Putting it together

When you look at any brand, or any business trying to earn value beyond basic usefulness, the four sections follow one path:

Proof → Shared recognition → Willingness to pay

Throughout this process, organisational decisions help sustain the meaning behind the brand.

Branding runs through all of it. It’s how a business communicates its proof, but it can only reinforce value the business has already earned.

This is a way to examine the process, not a guarantee that every brand follows it in order.

What makes the value a business claims believable to people beyond the business itself, and what must the organisation do to keep it believable?
